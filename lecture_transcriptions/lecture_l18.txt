Welcome back! Today in this video we are going to see an introduction to the proof. Proof obligations are the verification elements of your B model. Proving all these proof obligations is required to ensure the correctness of your model. Unfortunately, mathematical proof is a complicated subject and no tool exists or will never exist that assess the truth or untruth of all possible proof obligations. When a proof obligation is proved, we consider that it is true. When a proof obligation is not proved, we don't know if it is even false or if the proof tool is not smart or powerful enough to prove it. In this case, a visual inspection is required to quickly check if the in-proof proof obligations are true or not. Following the inspection, the B-models are likely to be modified if false proof obligations are formed or if proof obligations are too complex or automatic proof rate is too low. 

Once you are convinced that all the remaining proof obligations are true, you can start interactive proof. Interactive proof is an activity that is performed in interaction with the prover via a set of commands. The objective here is to find a sequence of commands, also called a proof script, that will demonstrate the target proof obligation. If the proof script is difficult to elaborate, or if it contains too many steps, the B model is likely to be modified to make the proof obligations easier to prove. 

Automatic proof is a push-button activity. Automatic proof is a quick process most of the time, especially as you can distribute automatic proof on all your cores. Below is a real-time capture of the automatic proof of the whole project.

The Atelier B main prover is a theorem prover. It contains more than 2500 rules. These rules may break down the current goal into smaller parts or parts easier to prove. They are called of type 1. In this example, proving A is a relation is equivalent to proving the domain of A belongs to the power set of S and the range of A belongs to the power set of T. Some rules are actions for the provers. For example, any interval p, q is a finite subset of integer. Or, A union B is equal to C is true if B union A is equal to C is an hypothesis. They may simplify predicates, goals or hypotheses of type 2. In this case, R of U of V is rewritten as R of V intersection with U. They may generate new hypotheses by combining them. 

Type 3. In this case, if a = b is a new hypothesis, and b = a an existing hypothesis, then a new hypothesis is generated a = b. By applying iteratively decomposition rules of type 1, the theorem prover creates a proof tree. A proof is completed when all the leaves of the proof tree turn to be axioms for the prover. An unproved proof obligation is represented by a proof tree where at least one leaf is not an axiom. To complete an incomplete interactive proof, you need to use interactive commands that add information at the location where the prover stops. We will see some of these commands. Or you can add your own mathematical rules, type 1, 2 or 3, that would handle the situation. But this is an advanced topic that will be covered in a future video.

A successful interactive demonstration is a combination of proof interactive commands and proof-or-execution. This demonstration, also called "proofed script", is saved and can be replayed at will to ensure that the proof obligation is true. 

Interactive proof is like doing a mathematical demonstration on the blackboard. You need to know why your proof obligation is true. You need to understand what you are proving and what parts of the B-models are involved. You need to know why it's not proved automatically. You need some experience with the proof tools and some knowledge about their strengths and weaknesses. And you need to know how to prove it.
